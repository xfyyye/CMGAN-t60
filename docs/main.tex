\begin{abstract}
Noise masks information about late reverberation decay, making blind reverberation time ($T_{60}$) estimation more difficult. This paper presents DACT-KAN (Denoising-Aided Conformer for T60 Estimation with a Fourier-KAN Head), a denoising-aided multi-task learning framework based on a shared time--frequency Conformer. The framework uses $T_{60}$ regression as the primary task and reconstruction of noise-free reverberant speech as the auxiliary task. The two branches receive the shared features in parallel and are jointly trained end to end. The regression branch aggregates time--frequency features through multi-statistic pooling and predicts $T_{60}$ using a two-layer Fourier-based Kolmogorov--Arnold network (Fourier-KAN). On four test sets covering simulated and measured room impulse responses and seen and unseen noise, the proposed model achieves lower root mean squared error (RMSE) and mean absolute error (MAE) than the three comparison methods. The corresponding averages across the four test sets are 92.32 ms and 55.08 ms, respectively. Ablation experiments show that, with the same multilayer perceptron (MLP) regression head, denoising-aided multi-task learning reduces the average RMSE by 10.8\% relative to single-task learning. In the multi-task setting, the Fourier-KAN regression head achieves a further 5.1\% reduction in average RMSE compared with an MLP regression head with a similar number of parameters and the same dropout rate.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

% END U009

% BEGIN U010

When a source emits sound in an enclosed space, the receiver captures both the direct sound and numerous reflections from the surrounding surfaces \citep{kuttruff2013room}. These reflections decay over time and together form room reverberation. Reverberation time, $T_{60}$, is defined as the time required for the sound energy to decay by 60 dB after the source stops emitting sound \citep{iso3382_2_2008}. As a scalar summary of room energy decay, $T_{60}$ is one of the most widely used parameters for characterising the acoustic properties of enclosed spaces. It directly affects speech quality and intelligibility, as well as the performance of downstream audio applications, including dereverberation, speech recognition, sound source localisation, and virtual/augmented reality rendering \citep{benzeghiba2007asrreview,anthes2016vr}. Standardised methods for measuring $T_{60}$ include the interrupted-noise method and the integrated impulse response method \citep{schroeder1965rt}. Both require an omnidirectional sound source, calibrated microphones, and trained operators. However, these measurement setups lack portability, and large-scale or in situ measurements are costly and complex to carry out. This has motivated \emph{blind} estimation methods that infer $T_{60}$ directly from recorded speech without dedicated excitation signals or prior knowledge of room geometry \citep{eaton2016ace}.

% END U010

% BEGIN U011

Blind $T_{60}$ estimation has been extensively studied over the past two decades. Traditional methods rely on statistical signal processing. Model-based estimators represent the late part of the room impulse response (RIR) as a damped Gaussian process \citep{polack1988these} and recover $T_{60}$ from the decay rate of its energy envelope. Feature-mapping estimators instead derive $T_{60}$ from handcrafted acoustic descriptors, such as the negative-side variance of decay rates, the pitch strength of harmonic components, or the spectral decay distribution \citep{ratnam2003blind,wen2008negativeside,eaton2013sdd,falk2010temporal,lollmann2010iwaenc}. The ACE Challenge systematically evaluated these methods \citep{eaton2016ace} and exposed two recurring difficulties: (i) most estimators exhibit a positive bias under additive noise because the noise-like reverberation tail is buried in the noise; and (ii) analytical decay models are particularly vulnerable under non-stationary or low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) conditions. Even variants with improved noise robustness, such as the computationally less demanding spectral decay distribution estimator \citep{eaton2013sdd} or the subband decomposition estimator \citep{prego2015subband}, still require a reasonably accurate prior estimate of the noise level. Obtaining such an estimate is itself difficult when the noise is non-stationary. Thus, masking of the reverberation tail by additive noise is a central obstacle to blind $T_{60}$ estimation. Recovering the decay structure robustly under noisy conditions remains a recurring challenge in subsequent work.

% END U011

% BEGIN U012

Deep learning subsequently emerged as a data-driven alternative. \citet{gamper2018cnn} used log-energy features from gammatone ERB subbands as input to a six-layer convolutional neural network (CNN) and evaluated its performance in blind reverberation time estimation on the ACE evaluation corpus. \citet{xiong2018ercnn} formulated joint estimation of $T_{60}$ and the early-to-late reverberation ratio as a classification problem using time--frequency features. \citet{deng2020crnn} introduced a convolutional recurrent network for online estimation from variable-length speech, and \citet{srivastava2021blindroom} extended this framework to multichannel inputs. \citet{goetz2023online} used three convolutional recurrent variants to estimate parameters under dynamic acoustic conditions. \citet{saini2023mobileaudiotransformer} demonstrated that a MobileViT-style transformer could run in real time on a smartphone. \citet{duangpummet2022stochastic} extended the scope from full-band $T_{60}$ estimation to the joint estimation of $T_{60}$, early decay time, clarity, and the speech transmission index. Despite their architectural differences, most of these models use the same regressor design: a time--frequency feature extraction network is followed by several fully connected layers that map the aggregated features to a scalar $T_{60}$. To improve robustness in noise, previous studies have combined denoising with $T_{60}$ estimation. \citet{zheng2022noiseaware} proposed a two-stage noise-aware framework. A speech and noise separation network is first pretrained and then jointly optimised with the $T_{60}$ estimation network in the second stage. The estimation network uses the original noisy magnitude spectrum together with the estimated magnitude spectra of speech and noise. \citet{zhang2024damtl} proposed DAMTL, a denoising-aided multi-task learning method for joint end-to-end learning of denoising reconstruction and $T_{60}$ regression. The method uses a shared feature extraction network containing complex-valued convolutions and complex-valued LSTMs.

% END U012

% BEGIN U013

Previous studies indicate that denoising-aided learning can improve blind $T_{60}$ estimation in noise. However, the late reverberation tail has low energy and is readily masked by noise, making it difficult to extract information about reverberation decay. Feature learning therefore needs to preserve time--frequency information associated with reverberation decay while suppressing noise interference.

% END U013

% BEGIN U014

To this end, we construct DACT-KAN, a multi-task framework with a shared time--frequency Conformer feature extraction network. Its denoising reconstruction branch and $T_{60}$ regression branch receive the shared features in parallel. The auxiliary denoising task targets noise-free reverberant speech and contributes to shared feature learning through reconstruction supervision at each time--frequency bin. The $T_{60}$ branch aggregates the shared features using multi-statistic pooling and employs a Fourier-KAN regression head to map the aggregated features nonlinearly to a scalar $T_{60}$.

% END U014

% BEGIN U015

The main contributions of this paper are as follows.

% END U015

% BEGIN U016

\begin{enumerate}
\item We construct a denoising-aided blind $T_{60}$ estimation framework based on a shared time--frequency Conformer, with $T_{60}$ regression as the primary task and reconstruction of noise-free reverberant speech as the auxiliary task. End-to-end joint training optimises the shared feature extraction network using both tasks.
\item We construct a two-layer Fourier-KAN $T_{60}$ regression head that maps the shared features obtained through multi-statistic pooling to a scalar $T_{60}$. Experiments show that, in the multi-task setting examined, this head achieves lower estimation error than an MLP regression head with a similar number of parameters and the same dropout rate.
\item We compare the proposed framework with a single-task baseline and methods including BERP, DAMTL, and noise-aware on four test sets covering simulated and measured RIRs and seen and unseen noise. We evaluate its estimation performance under different test conditions using results grouped by SNR and by $T_{60}$ interval, together with ablation experiments.
\end{enumerate}

% END U016

% BEGIN U017

\section{Signal Model}
\label{sec:signal_model}

% END U019

% BEGIN U020

\subsection{Acoustic Signal Model}
\label{sec:acoustic_model}

% END U020

% BEGIN U021

Let $s(t)$ denote the anechoic speech emitted by a sound source at a fixed position, $h(t)$ the room impulse response (RIR) from the source to the microphone, and $n(t)$ the uncorrelated additive noise captured at the microphone. In the time domain, the recorded noisy reverberant signal $y(t)$ is modelled as

% END U021

% BEGIN U022

\begin{equation}
y(t) = s(t)\ast h(t) + n(t), \tag{1}\label{eq:signal}
\end{equation}

% END U022

% BEGIN U023

where $\ast$ denotes convolution. Let $r(t)=s(t)\ast h(t)$ denote the noise-free reverberant speech and $R(k,l)$ its short-time Fourier transform (STFT). By the linearity of the STFT, the observed complex spectrum satisfies $Y(k,l)=R(k,l)+N(k,l)$, where $k$ and $l$ index frequency bins and time frames, respectively, and $N(k,l)$ is the complex spectrum of the noise.

% END U023

% BEGIN U024

Blind $T_{60}$ estimation aims to learn the mapping $\mathcal{F}:Y \mapsto \hat{T}_{60}$. At inference, predictions are made solely from the recorded signal, without requiring the anechoic speech, the RIR, or the noise component. Here, $Y(k,l)$ is the uncompressed observed complex spectrum. In Section~\ref{sec:proposed}, $\mathbf Y$ denotes the complex spectrum obtained by applying magnitude compression to the STFT of the normalised waveform.

% END U024

% BEGIN U025

\subsection{Reverberation Time and the Polack Model}
\label{sec:polack}

% END U025

% BEGIN U026

Estimating $T_{60}$ from the observed signal $y(t)$ requires an understanding of the relationship between $T_{60}$ and the structure of the RIR $h(t)$. Under the idealised assumption of a single exponential decay in late diffuse reverberation, the Polack model \citep{polack1988these} describes the late RIR as a zero-mean damped Gaussian process. In what follows, the start of the late segment is taken as the time origin, and the notation $h(t)$ is retained for the response within that segment:

% END U026

% BEGIN U027

\begin{equation}
h(t) = b(t)\, e^{-\delta t},\qquad t\geq 0, \tag{2}\label{eq:polack}
\end{equation}

% END U027

% BEGIN U028

where $b(t)$ is a zero-mean stationary white-noise process with variance $\sigma_b^{2}$, and $\delta>0$ is the room damping constant. The second-order statistics of the model in Eq.~\eqref{eq:polack} give the following decay law for the mean-square energy envelope:

% END U028

% BEGIN U029

\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}\{|h(t)|^{2}\} = \sigma_b^{2}\,e^{-2\delta t}, \tag{3}\label{eq:energy_decay}
\end{equation}

% END U029

% BEGIN U030

Using the definition of a 60 dB energy decay, namely $10\log_{10} e^{-2\delta T_{60}} = -60$, we obtain the following analytical expression for reverberation time:

% END U030

% BEGIN U031

\begin{equation}
T_{60} = \frac{3\ln 10}{\delta}. \tag{4}\label{eq:t60_decay}
\end{equation}

% END U031

% BEGIN U032

Model-based estimators fit the decay rate $\delta$ through linear regression on the logarithm of the estimated energy envelope. When reverberant speech is contaminated by noise, however, the envelope is no longer purely exponential. The estimate of $\delta$ becomes severely biased, usually leading to an overestimate of $T_{60}$ \citep{gaubitch2012comparison}. This limitation motivates data-driven methods that directly learn the mapping from $Y(k,l)$ to $\hat{T}_{60}$.

% END U032

\section{Proposed Method}
\label{sec:proposed}

% END U034

% BEGIN U035

\subsection{Proposed Multi-Task Framework}
\label{sec:framework}

% END U035

% BEGIN U036

As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:framework}, we construct a multi-task framework with $T_{60}$ estimation as the primary task and denoising reconstruction as the auxiliary task. The noisy reverberant speech is first transformed using the STFT. Shared time--frequency features are then extracted by a dense convolutional encoder and two cascaded time--frequency Conformer blocks (TSCBs). The network then splits into a denoising branch and a $T_{60}$ regression branch, which perform spectral reconstruction and reverberation time estimation, respectively.

% END U036

% BEGIN U037

The denoising branch reconstructs the spectrum using magnitude masking and complex residuals \citep{cao2022cmgan}. The reconstructed spectrum is then converted to denoised speech using the inverse STFT (iSTFT). Its reconstruction target is noise-free reverberant speech, so that additive noise is removed while reverberation is retained. The $T_{60}$ branch takes the shared features directly as input and estimates reverberation time through multi-statistic pooling and a Fourier-KAN regression head. The denoised speech output is not an input to this branch.

% END U037

% BEGIN U038

During training, both tasks update the shared feature extraction network, providing supervision from spectral reconstruction and $T_{60}$ regression. The parameters specific to each branch are optimised by the corresponding task loss. Figure~\ref{fig:framework} shows only the overall modules and their input--output relationships; the internal structures of the encoder, TSCBs, regression head, and denoising decoders are described in the following sections.

% END U038

% BEGIN U039

\begin{figure*}[pos=t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figs_chapter/proposed_multitask_framework_v2.png}
  \caption{Proposed multi-task framework. The noisy reverberant speech is first processed using the STFT and the shared feature extraction network. The resulting features are fed in parallel to the upper auxiliary denoising branch and the lower primary $T_{60}$ estimation branch.}
  \label{fig:framework}
\end{figure*}

\subsection{Dense Convolutional Encoder}
\label{sec:encoder}

% END U041

% BEGIN U042

The dense convolutional encoder converts noisy reverberant speech into features for subsequent time--frequency modelling and serves as the input stage of the feature extraction network shared by both tasks. A single-channel input waveform $y[n]$ of length $L$ is first energy-normalised:

% END U042

% BEGIN U043

\begin{equation}
c=\sqrt{\frac{L}{\sum_{n=0}^{L-1}y^2[n]+\varepsilon}},
\qquad
\tilde y[n]=c\,y[n], \tag{5}\label{eq:energy_norm}
\end{equation}

% END U043

% BEGIN U044

where $\varepsilon>0$ is a numerical stabilisation term. The normalised waveform is transformed into a complex spectrum using the STFT with a Hamming window. Its magnitude is then raised to a fixed power $\gamma$ ($0<\gamma<1$), while the phase remains unchanged. Denote the resulting compressed complex spectrum by $\mathbf Y\in\mathbb C^{T\times F}$, where $T$ and $F$ are the numbers of time frames and frequency bins, respectively. Its magnitude, real part, and imaginary part are stacked along the channel dimension to form the three-channel encoder input:

% END U044

% BEGIN U045

\begin{equation}
\mathbf X_{\mathrm{in}}
=
\bigl[
|\mathbf Y|,
\operatorname{Re}(\mathbf Y),
\operatorname{Im}(\mathbf Y)
\bigr]
\in\mathbb R^{3\times T\times F}. \tag{6}\label{eq:encoder_input}
\end{equation}

% END U045

% BEGIN U046

The encoder consists of channel projection, dilated dense encoding, and frequency downsampling \citep{cao2022cmgan}. As shown in the upper part of Fig.~\ref{fig:encoder}, a $1\times1$ pointwise convolution first maps the three input channels to $C$ feature channels, followed by instance normalisation and a parametric ReLU (PReLU) activation. This stage preserves the time and frequency dimensions, and its output is passed to a dilated dense block.

% END U046

% BEGIN U047

The lower part of Fig.~\ref{fig:encoder} shows the internal structure of the dilated dense block in more detail. The block contains four $2\times3$ convolutional layers, with the two kernel dimensions corresponding to time and frequency, respectively. The four layers have temporal dilation rates of $1,2,4,8$, respectively, and a frequency dilation rate of $1$. Zero-padding preserves the time--frequency dimensions, and each convolution is followed by instance normalisation and PReLU. Each layer takes as input the channel-wise concatenation of the block input and all preceding layer outputs, and produces a feature map with $C$ channels for use by subsequent layers. The block returns the features produced by its fourth layer. The concatenated features are used for internal connections between layers.

% END U047

% BEGIN U048

The block output then passes through a $1\times3$ convolution with a temporal stride of $1$ and a frequency stride of $2$, downsampling only along the frequency axis. Instance normalisation and PReLU follow this convolution. The encoder output is thus

% END U048

% BEGIN U049

\begin{equation}
\mathbf F^{(0)}
=
\operatorname{Encoder}(\mathbf X_{\mathrm{in}})
\in\mathbb R^{C\times T\times F'},
\qquad
F'=\left\lceil\frac F2\right\rceil. \tag{7}\label{eq:encoder_output}
\end{equation}

% END U049

% BEGIN U050

\begin{figure}[pos=htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figs_chapter/denoise_encoder_v6.png}
  \caption{Dense convolutional encoder. The upper part shows input projection, the dilated dense block, and frequency downsampling; the lower part shows the four convolutional layers with temporal dilation rates of 1, 2, 4, and 8. A circled C denotes channel-wise concatenation, and the dense block returns the output of the fourth layer.}
  \label{fig:encoder}
\end{figure}

% END U050

% BEGIN U052

\subsection{Time--Frequency Conformer Block}
\label{sec:tscb}

% END U052

% BEGIN U053

The encoder output $\mathbf F^{(0)}\in\mathbb R^{C\times T\times F'}$ is then passed through $N=2$ cascaded time--frequency Conformer blocks (TSCBs). Each TSCB applies a temporal Conformer followed by a frequency Conformer to process the time--frequency features along the two axes while preserving the input dimensions \citep{cao2022cmgan}.

% END U053

% BEGIN U054

As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:tscb}(a), the temporal stage arranges the features at each frequency position into a sequence of length $T$ with feature dimension $C$. The temporal Conformer processes this sequence, and its output is added element-wise to the corresponding input. The frequency stage then rearranges the updated features at each time frame into a sequence of length $F'$ with feature dimension $C$. The frequency Conformer processes this sequence, and its output is likewise added element-wise to the corresponding input. The Conformers in the two stages have the same architecture but independent parameters. After the frequency stage, the features are rearranged into a $C\times T\times F'$ layout to form the TSCB output.

% END U054

% BEGIN U055

Figure~\ref{fig:tscb}(b) shows the internal structure of a single Conformer in more detail. Its four submodules sequentially update the sequence representation through residual connections \citep{gulati2020conformer}. For an input sequence $\mathbf Z$, the computation can be written as

% END U055

% BEGIN U056

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf Z_1
&=\mathbf Z+\tfrac12\operatorname{FFN}_1(\mathbf Z),\\
\mathbf Z_2
&=\mathbf Z_1+\operatorname{MHSA}(\mathbf Z_1),\\
\mathbf Z_3
&=\mathbf Z_2+\operatorname{Conv}(\mathbf Z_2),\\
\mathbf Z_4
&=\mathbf Z_3+\tfrac12\operatorname{FFN}_2(\mathbf Z_3),\\
\mathcal C(\mathbf Z)
&=\operatorname{LN}_{\mathrm{out}}(\mathbf Z_4).
\end{aligned} \tag{8}\label{eq:conformer}
\end{equation}

% END U056

% BEGIN U057

Here, $\mathrm{FFN}_1$ and $\mathrm{FFN}_2$ are two pre-normalised position-wise feed-forward modules with Swish activations; $\mathrm{MHSA}$ is a multi-head self-attention module with Shaw-style relative positional encoding \citep{shaw2018relativepos}; and $\mathrm{Conv}$ is a depthwise separable one-dimensional convolution module with a gated linear unit and batch normalisation. The self-attention and convolution modules are placed between the two feed-forward modules. Both feed-forward residual branches use a scaling factor of $1/2$, forming a Macaron-style structure. The $\mathrm{MHSA}$ and $\mathrm{Conv}$ modules also include layer normalisation at their respective inputs, and $\mathrm{LN}_{\mathrm{out}}$ denotes the final layer normalisation of the Conformer.

% END U057

% BEGIN U058

The same internal structure operates along the time axis in the temporal stage and along the frequency axis in the frequency stage. After these internal operations and the final layer normalisation, the Conformer output is added element-wise to the stage input through the external residual connection shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:tscb}(a).

% END U058

% BEGIN U059

The two cascaded TSCBs yield shared time--frequency features $\mathbf F^{(N)}\in\mathbb R^{C\times T\times F'}$. These features are then passed in parallel to the $T_{60}$ regression branch (Section~\ref{sec:t60_head}) and the denoising decoder branch (Section~\ref{sec:denoise}) for reverberation time estimation and speech reconstruction, respectively.

% END U059

% BEGIN U060

\begin{figure}[pos=htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figs_chapter/ts_conformer_v4.png}
  \caption{Time--frequency Conformer block (TSCB). (a) The temporal and frequency Conformers process the shared features sequentially, with an external residual connection at each stage. (b) The feed-forward, self-attention, and convolution modules within a single Conformer and their residual connections. The temporal and frequency Conformers have the same architecture but independent parameters.}
  \label{fig:tscb}
\end{figure}

% END U060

% BEGIN U062

\subsection{Fourier-KAN-Based $T_{60}$ Regression Branch}
\label{sec:t60_head}

% END U062

% BEGIN U063

The $T_{60}$ regression branch maps the time--frequency features from the shared backbone to a reverberation time estimate for each input sample, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:t60_head}(a). Let the shared features be $\mathbf F^{(N)}\in\mathbb R^{C\times T\times F'}$, where $C$, $T$, and $F'$ denote the number of channels, the number of time frames, and the size of the frequency dimension, respectively. The batch dimension is omitted below. To obtain a sample-level feature representation, the features are first averaged along the time dimension:

% END U063

% BEGIN U064

\begin{equation}
\overline F_{c,f}
=
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}F^{(N)}_{c,t,f}, \tag{9}\label{eq:time_pool}
\end{equation}

% END U064

% BEGIN U065

This yields $\overline{\mathbf F}\in\mathbb R^{C\times F'}$. The mean, maximum, and standard deviation are then computed along the frequency dimension within each channel and concatenated into a fixed-dimensional vector:

% END U065

% BEGIN U066

\begin{equation}
\mathbf z
=
\operatorname{Concat}
\left[
\operatorname{Mean}_{f}(\overline{\mathbf F}),
\operatorname{Max}_{f}(\overline{\mathbf F}),
\operatorname{Std}_{f}(\overline{\mathbf F})
\right]
\in\mathbb R^{3C}. \tag{10}\label{eq:stats_pool}
\end{equation}

% END U066

% BEGIN U067

The vector is passed through a linear projection followed by layer normalisation to form the input to Fourier-KAN:

% END U067

% BEGIN U068

\begin{equation}
\mathbf h^{(0)}
=
\operatorname{LN}_{0}
\left(\mathbf W_{\mathrm p}\mathbf z+\mathbf b_{\mathrm p}\right)
\in\mathbb R^{d_0}, \tag{11}\label{eq:projection}
\end{equation}

% END U068

% BEGIN U069

where $\mathbf W_{\mathrm p}$ and $\mathbf b_{\mathrm p}$ are the projection parameters, $d_0$ is the projected feature dimension, and $\operatorname{LN}$ denotes layer normalisation.

% END U069

% BEGIN U070

KAN places learnable univariate functions on the edges connecting nodes and constructs a multidimensional mapping by aggregating the function outputs on these edges \citep{liu2024kan}. We use Fourier basis functions to parameterise these edge functions and construct a Fourier-KAN regression mapping \citep{xu2025fourierkan}. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:t60_head}(b), each edge transforms its scalar input, and each output node sums the function values from all incoming edges and adds a node bias. For layer $\ell$, the aggregated value at node $j$ is

% END U070

% BEGIN U071

\begin{equation}
u_j^{(\ell)}
=
\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\ell-1}}
\phi_{ji}^{(\ell)}
\left(h_i^{(\ell-1)}\right)
+
b_j^{(\ell)},
\qquad j=1,\ldots,d_\ell, \tag{12}\label{eq:kan_aggregate}
\end{equation}

% END U071

% BEGIN U072

where $d_{\ell-1}$ and $d_\ell$ are the input and output dimensions of the layer, respectively, $\phi_{ji}^{(\ell)}$ is the edge function from input node $i$ to output node $j$, and $b_j^{(\ell)}$ is the node bias.

% END U072

% BEGIN U073

Figure~\ref{fig:t60_head}(c) shows how the function on the highlighted edge in Fig.~\ref{fig:t60_head}(b) is constructed. Each edge function is a weighted combination of a finite number of sine and cosine basis functions:

% END U073

% BEGIN U074

\begin{equation}
\phi_{ji}^{(\ell)}(x)
=
\sum_{k=1}^{K_\ell}
\left[
a_{jik}^{(\ell)}\cos(kx)
+
c_{jik}^{(\ell)}\sin(kx)
\right], \tag{13}\label{eq:fourier_edge}
\end{equation}

% END U074

% BEGIN U075

Here, $x$ is the scalar input to the edge function, and $K_\ell$ is the Fourier expansion order of layer $\ell$. The index $k$ takes the fixed integer values $1,\ldots,K_\ell$. The learnable coefficients $a_{jik}^{(\ell)}$ and $c_{jik}^{(\ell)}$ weight the cosine and sine terms, respectively. The shape of the edge function is therefore determined by these basis coefficients and changes as the coefficients are updated during training.

% END U075

% BEGIN U076

The regression branch cascades two Fourier-KAN layers. After edge-function transformations and node aggregation, each layer applies layer normalisation followed by dropout:

% END U076

% BEGIN U077

\begin{equation}
\mathbf h^{(\ell)}
=
\operatorname{Dropout}_{\ell}
\left(
\operatorname{LN}_{\ell}
\left(\mathbf u^{(\ell)}\right)
\right),
\qquad \ell=1,2. \tag{14}\label{eq:kan_post}
\end{equation}

% END U077

% BEGIN U078

The second-layer output $\mathbf h^{(2)}\in\mathbb R^{d_2}$ is passed through a linear mapping and a sigmoid function to obtain the normalised $T_{60}$ prediction:

% END U078

% BEGIN U079

\begin{equation}
\hat{T}_{60}^{\mathrm{norm}}
=
\sigma\left(
\mathbf w_{\mathrm o}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf h^{(2)}
+
b_{\mathrm o}
\right)
\in(0,1), \tag{15}\label{eq:t60_predict}
\end{equation}

% END U079

% BEGIN U080

where $\mathbf w_{\mathrm o}$ and $b_{\mathrm o}$ are the output-layer parameters, and $\sigma(\cdot)$ denotes the sigmoid function.

\subsection{Denoising Decoder Branch}
\label{sec:denoise}

% END U083

% BEGIN U084

The denoising decoder branch receives the shared time--frequency features $\mathbf F^{(N)}$ and uses noise-free reverberant speech $\tilde s(t)=s(t)\ast h(t)$ as the reconstruction target, providing auxiliary supervision for the shared feature extraction network. The target waveform and noisy input share the energy normalisation coefficient computed from the input (Eq.~\eqref{eq:energy_norm}) and undergo the same STFT and power-law compression described in Section~\ref{sec:encoder}. Below, $\mathbf Y$ denotes the compressed complex input spectrum, and $\tilde S$ denotes the compressed complex target spectrum.

% END U084

% BEGIN U085

This branch uses parallel magnitude-mask and complex-residual decoding paths \citep{cao2022cmgan}. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:denoise}, the two paths process the shared features using dilated dense blocks with independent parameters. Frequency-axis sub-pixel upsampling and convolutional mappings then restore the frequency dimension from $F'$ to $F$ while preserving the number of time frames $T$. The upper mask path outputs a single-channel real-valued mask $M$ and ends with a PReLU activation parameterised separately for each frequency. The lower complex path outputs a two-channel residual $\Delta$, whose channels correspond to the real and imaginary parts; no activation is applied at its output layer. The two mappings are expressed as

% END U085

% BEGIN U086

\begin{equation}
M=\mathcal D_{\mathrm{mask}}\!\left(\mathbf F^{(N)}\right),
\qquad
\Delta=\mathcal D_{\mathrm{complex}}\!\left(\mathbf F^{(N)}\right)
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\Delta_{\mathrm{re}}\\
\Delta_{\mathrm{im}}
\end{bmatrix}, \tag{16}\label{eq:decoder_outputs}
\end{equation}

% END U086

% BEGIN U087

where $\mathcal D_{\mathrm{mask}}$ and $\mathcal D_{\mathrm{complex}}$ denote the mask decoder and complex-residual decoder, respectively.

% END U087

% BEGIN U088

The right-hand side of Fig.~\ref{fig:denoise} shows how the two path outputs are combined. The mask is first multiplied element-wise by the compressed input magnitude to obtain the masked magnitude $\hat A=M\odot|\mathbf Y|$. This magnitude is then combined with the input phase to form an initial complex spectrum. The residuals from the complex path are then added to the real and imaginary parts of this spectrum, respectively:

% END U088

% BEGIN U089

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\hat Y_{\mathrm{re}}
&=
\hat A\odot\cos\!\left(\angle\mathbf Y\right)
+\Delta_{\mathrm{re}},\\
\hat Y_{\mathrm{im}}
&=
\hat A\odot\sin\!\left(\angle\mathbf Y\right)
+\Delta_{\mathrm{im}},
\end{aligned} \tag{17}\label{eq:complex_reconstruction}
\end{equation}

% END U089

% BEGIN U090

Here, $\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication, and $\angle\mathbf Y$ is the input phase. This gives the predicted compressed complex spectrum $\hat{\mathbf Y}=\hat Y_{\mathrm{re}}+j\hat Y_{\mathrm{im}}$: the mask path adjusts the input magnitude component, while the complex-residual path further corrects the reconstructed real and imaginary parts.

% END U090

% BEGIN U091

Both the multiplication and residual addition are performed in the power-compressed complex spectral domain. The power-law compression of the magnitude of $\hat{\mathbf Y}$ is first inverted. The iSTFT is then applied to obtain the denoised waveform $\hat s(t)$. Reconstruction errors for the predicted spectrum and waveform, each relative to its corresponding target, contribute to network optimisation through the joint loss defined in Section~\ref{sec:loss}.

% END U091

% BEGIN U092

\begin{figure}[pos=htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figs_chapter/denoise_decoder_v4.png}
  \caption{Denoising decoder branch. Shared TSCB features are passed to the magnitude-mask and complex-residual decoders. The mask is multiplied element-wise by the noisy magnitude, and the resulting magnitude is combined with the noisy phase to reconstruct an initial complex spectrum. The complex residual is then added element-wise to this spectrum. The multiplication and addition nodes represent these two operations, respectively; the two channels of the complex residual correspond to its real and imaginary parts. The illustrated operations take place in the power-compressed domain, with inverse compression and the iSTFT applied after complex spectrum reconstruction.}
  \label{fig:denoise}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Loss Functions}
\label{sec:loss}

% END U094

% BEGIN U095

To match the sigmoid output range in Eq.~\eqref{eq:t60_predict}, each ground-truth $T_{60}$ is linearly mapped to $[0,1]$ before the loss is computed:

% END U095

% BEGIN U096

\begin{equation}
T_{60}^{\mathrm{norm}} = \frac{T_{60} - T_{\min}}{T_{\max} - T_{\min}}. \tag{18}\label{eq:label_norm}
\end{equation}

% END U096

% BEGIN U097

The $T_{60}$ regression loss is the mean square error between the predicted and ground-truth normalised $T_{60}$ values over a mini-batch of $B$ utterances:

% END U097

% BEGIN U098

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{T_{60}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\bigl(\hat{T}_{60,i}^{\mathrm{norm}} - T_{60,i}^{\mathrm{norm}}\bigr)^{2}. \tag{19}\label{eq:t60_loss}
\end{equation}

% END U098

% BEGIN U099

The denoising loss combines real/imaginary-part and magnitude losses in the compressed domain with a time-domain loss:

% END U099

% BEGIN U100

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{den}} = w_{ri}\,\mathcal{L}_{ri} + w_{mag}\,\mathcal{L}_{mag} + w_{time}\,\mathcal{L}_{time}, \tag{20}\label{eq:denoise_loss}
\end{equation}

% END U100

% BEGIN U101

Here, $\mathcal{L}_{ri}$ is the sum of the real-part and imaginary-part mean square errors between the predicted compressed complex spectrum and the target $\tilde{S}$, and $\mathcal{L}_{mag}$ is the mean square error between their magnitudes. The time-domain loss $\mathcal{L}_{time}$ is the mean absolute error between the enhanced waveform $\hat s$ and the normalised target $c\,\tilde s$, with $\hat s$ obtained after inverse compression and the iSTFT. The noise-free reverberant speech is $\tilde s=s\ast h$; for each utterance, the target and noisy input share the normalisation coefficient $c$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:energy_norm}. The compressed complex target spectrum $\tilde{S}$ is obtained by applying the same STFT and power-law compression to this normalised target.

% END U101

% BEGIN U102

To reduce the difference in numerical scale between the denoising and $T_{60}$ losses, each loss is scaled using its own exponential moving average (EMA). Let $q\in\{\mathrm{den},T_{60}\}$ denote the task and $u$ index mini-batch calls to the loss function. Then,

% END U102

% BEGIN U103

\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
m_{q,1}&=\operatorname{stopgrad}(\mathcal{L}_{q,1}),\\
m_{q,u}&=\rho\,m_{q,u-1}+(1-\rho)\operatorname{stopgrad}(\mathcal{L}_{q,u}),\qquad u>1,\\
\mathcal{L}_{q,u}^{*}&=\frac{\mathcal{L}_{q,u}}{m_{q,u}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{EMA}}}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}

% END U103

% BEGIN U104

where $\rho=0.99$, $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{EMA}}=10^{-8}$, and $\operatorname{stopgrad}(\cdot)$ denotes the stop-gradient operation. The EMA is initialised directly with the current loss on the first batch. On every call, the EMA is updated before the loss is normalised by the updated value; no gradients are propagated through the denominator. In the implementation, training and validation share the same EMA states, which are updated per mini-batch in both phases and retained continuously across phase transitions and successive epochs. No backpropagation or model parameter updates are performed during validation.

% END U104

% BEGIN U105

This scaling adjusts the numerical scales of the two losses. The total loss is

% END U105

% BEGIN U106

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \alpha\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{den}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) + \beta\,\mathcal{L}_{T_{60}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta}), \tag{21}\label{eq:joint_loss}
\end{equation}

% END U106

% BEGIN U107

where $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ comprises all trainable parameters of the shared encoder and TSCBs, the Fourier-KAN head, and the two denoising decoders. The loss weights and main model configuration are given in Section~\ref{sec:settings}.

% END U107

% BEGIN U108

At inference, the sigmoid output is denormalised using the inverse of Eq.~\eqref{eq:label_norm}, yielding the final estimate $\hat{T}_{60}\in[T_{\min}, T_{\max}]$.

% END U108

% BEGIN U124

BERP \citep{wang2025berp} is a general framework for blind room parameter estimation. It extracts room features using a shared encoder that combines attention mechanisms and convolutional layers and uses separate predictors to estimate different room parameters. Only its $T_{60}$ predictions are evaluated here. We use BERP as a baseline that learns room features from noisy reverberant speech for parameter regression.

% END U124

% BEGIN U125

DAMTL \citep{zhang2024damtl} uses denoising as an auxiliary task and jointly learns denoising reconstruction and $T_{60}$ estimation to improve estimation accuracy in noise. Its primary and auxiliary tasks are similar to ours, making it a baseline for direct comparison of denoising-aided multi-task frameworks. The archived training records for this baseline document a batch size of 16, an initial learning rate of $5\times10^{-4}$, a maximum of 100 epochs, and checkpoint selection based on the validation $T_{60}$ loss.

% END U125

% BEGIN U126

The noise-aware time--frequency masking method \citep{zheng2022noiseaware} uses a serial two-stage processing architecture. A separation network first estimates the magnitude spectra of noise-free reverberant speech and noise. An estimation network then predicts $T_{60}$ using these two estimates together with the magnitude spectrum of the original noisy speech. The separation network is pretrained first; the separation and estimation networks are then jointly optimised. This method provides a comparison between serial noise separation and our shared-feature, parallel-branch architecture.

% END U126

% BEGIN U127

The single-task Fourier-KAN baseline retains the same feature extraction network and regression head but removes the denoising branch. It is trained solely using the mean square error between its predictions and the normalised $T_{60}$ labels.

\subsubsection{$T_{60}$ Estimation Metrics}
\label{sec:t60_metrics}

% END U128

% BEGIN U129

\textbf{Root mean squared error (RMSE).} RMSE measures the overall discrepancy between estimated and ground-truth $T_{60}$ values, penalising larger deviations more heavily. It is defined as

% END U129

% BEGIN U130

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RMSE}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\hat{T}_{60,i}-T_{60,i})^{2}}, \tag{22}\label{eq:rmse}
\end{equation}

% END U130

% BEGIN U131

where $N$ is the number of samples, and $\hat{T}_{60,i}$ and $T_{60,i}$ are the estimated and ground-truth values for sample $i$, respectively.

% END U131

% BEGIN U132

\textbf{Mean absolute error (MAE).} MAE is the mean absolute deviation between estimated and ground-truth values over the test samples:

% END U132

% BEGIN U133

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MAE}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\hat{T}_{60,i}-T_{60,i}|, \tag{23}\label{eq:mae}
\end{equation}

% END U133

% BEGIN U134

Both RMSE and MAE are reported in milliseconds; lower values indicate smaller estimation errors.

% END U134

% BEGIN U135

\textbf{Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC).} PCC measures the linear correlation between estimated and ground-truth values and complements the error metrics. It ranges over $[-1,1]$, with values closer to 1 indicating a stronger positive linear correlation. It is calculated as

% END U135

% BEGIN U136

\begin{equation}
r=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\hat{T}_{60,i}-\bar{\hat{T}}_{60})(T_{60,i}-\bar{T}_{60})}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\hat{T}_{60,i}-\bar{\hat{T}}_{60})^{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(T_{60,i}-\bar{T}_{60})^{2}}}, \tag{24}\label{eq:pcc}
\end{equation}

% END U136

% BEGIN U137

where $\bar{\hat{T}}_{60}$ and $\bar{T}_{60}$ are the sample means of the estimated and ground-truth values, respectively.

% END U137

% BEGIN U138

\textbf{Coefficient of determination ($R^{2}$).} Using the sample mean of the ground-truth values as the reference predictor, $R^{2}$ evaluates the model's fit. Values closer to 1 indicate a better fit. It is defined as

% END U138

% BEGIN U139

\begin{equation}
R^{2}=1-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\hat{T}_{60,i}-T_{60,i})^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(T_{60,i}-\bar{T}_{60})^{2}}. \tag{25}\label{eq:r2}
\end{equation}

% END U139

% BEGIN U140

For the main comparison, metrics are calculated separately on each test set and then averaged arithmetically. RMSE is further analysed by SNR and by $T_{60}$ interval.

% END U140

% BEGIN U141

\subsubsection{Denoising Quality Metrics}
\label{sec:denoise_metrics}

% END U141

% BEGIN U142

Three commonly used objective metrics evaluate the quality of the denoised speech. Wideband PESQ (ITU-T P.862.2) \citep{itut2007p8622} evaluates perceptual speech quality. STOI \citep{taal2011stoi} evaluates speech intelligibility. The scale-invariant signal-to-distortion ratio (SI-SDR) \citep{leroux2019sisdr} evaluates signal reconstruction fidelity and is reported in dB. Higher values indicate better performance for all three metrics.

% END U142

% BEGIN U143

All metrics use noise-free reverberant speech as the reference and are calculated for both the noisy input and denoised output. For each metric, the improvement is defined as the output value minus the input value.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

% END U187

% BEGIN U188

This paper presents a multi-task learning framework for blind $T_{60}$ estimation, with $T_{60}$ regression as the primary task and denoising reconstruction as the auxiliary task. The tasks share a time--frequency Conformer feature extraction network, and a Fourier-KAN regression head estimates $T_{60}$. Across the four test sets, the proposed model achieves an average RMSE of 92.32 ms, 17.2\% lower than that of the single-task Fourier-KAN baseline. It also outperforms comparison methods including BERP, DAMTL, and noise-aware. Ablation experiments show that denoising-aided multi-task training reduces the MLP model's average RMSE by 10.8\% relative to single-task training. Under this multi-task setting, replacing the MLP regression head with a Fourier-KAN head that has a similar parameter count and the same dropout rate further reduces average RMSE by 5.1\%. Task-weight analysis further shows that joint training performance depends on the weight settings, with $(\alpha,\beta)=(1.0,0.05)$ performing best among the configurations examined.

% END U188

% BEGIN U189

Several extensions remain to be explored. First, in the long-reverberation interval of the current test data ($T_{60}\in[1.0,1.5]$ s), the estimation errors of the methods are generally higher than in the short-reverberation intervals. Although the proposed model reduces errors in this interval, there is still scope to improve accuracy. Future work could examine the effects of targeted data augmentation or sample reweighting on estimation performance under long-reverberation conditions. Second, this work focuses on single-channel inputs. The multi-task framework could be extended to multichannel scenarios or adapted for joint estimation of $T_{60}$ and other room acoustic parameters, such as early decay time and clarity.
